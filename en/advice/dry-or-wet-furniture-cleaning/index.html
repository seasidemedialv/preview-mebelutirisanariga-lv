Dry or wet furniture cleaning

Dry or wet furniture cleaning is not a matter of taste, because what suits a fabric is usually set by the care label. Code W means water-based foam, S a water-free solvent, WS both and X no liquid at all. Both approaches carry risks: with water, water rings and damp upholstery; with solvent, damaged foam. If there is no label, a test in a hidden spot is needed before any cleaning.

These are general conditions, not a description of our work. What suits your sofa depends on the fabric and the label, and a specialist assesses that from a photo or on site. People use the term “dry cleaning” for different things in everyday speech, so ask what it means in your particular case.

How to read a care label

The label is often hidden under the cushions or on the underside of the furniture. The codes are voluntary, so some furniture has none.

  • W: stains are cleaned with water-based foam, sparingly, without excessive wetting. Dry-cleaning solvents are not allowed.
  • S: only a mild water-free solvent or a dry-cleaning product, in a well-ventilated room, tested beforehand. Professional cleaning is recommended.
  • WS or S-W: solvent or foam from a mild washing solution, tested first in both cases.
  • X: no liquid at all, only vacuuming and light brushing; any other cleaning has to be decided separately.

Sources explain code X differently: some say “vacuum only”, others “do not clean without a professional”. So here we use the most cautious wording. Sources: Virginia Cooperative Extension, American Leather, checked 07.11.2026.

Do not confuse the codes with care symbols. A letter W in a circle on a textile care label means professional wet cleaning, and that is a different system. In the GINETEX symbol system a cross through a symbol means the treatment must not be used, while letters in a circle indicate solvents and processes for professional cleaning.

What happens if you choose wrongly

Too much water can move the fabric finish and leave a water ring. The fabric may shrink, the colour may change or bleed, and moisture may reach the upholstery. Water and pressure can crush viscose flock irreversibly. Foam helps avoid shrinkage and colour changes better than liquid does.

With solvent the risk is different. The University of Georgia warns that solvents can damage foam upholstery. Some faux leather and other leather imitations do not withstand dry cleaning: the film or glue may deteriorate, and bubbles or wrinkles may appear. This information is about clothing, so we apply it to sofas with caution.

There are even more special rules for leather: can a leather sofa be cleaned. A mattress has a separate limit, because the core is hard to dry: how often to clean a mattress.

What to do if there is no label or it cannot be read

  1. Look for it. Under the cushions, on the underside of the furniture and in the manufacturer's documents.
  2. Identify the fabric. If you know whether it is velour, chenille, cotton or faux leather, tell us.
  3. Test in a hidden spot. If you are going to use something, first try a small amount in a hidden spot and see whether the colour changes.
  4. Send a photo. There is no general rule for microfibre and flock, so the actual fabric has to be seen: microfibre and flock sofa cleaning.

Frequently asked questions

Which kind of cleaning is better?

There is no single best one. Fabric with code W does not allow solvents, fabric with code S does not allow water, and X does not allow liquids at all. The better one is the one the label allows.

What method do you use?

We do not name it here, because the choice depends on the fabric and the label. Send a photo and a specialist will tell you what to expect in your particular case.

Does W on a label mean the fabric can be washed?

No. It means cleaning stains with water-based foam, sparingly. Washing or soaking is not what it refers to.

Can cushion covers be washed to avoid wetting the sofa?

It is usually better not to. A zipped cover may shrink or change colour, and it may end up looking different from the rest of the furniture.
